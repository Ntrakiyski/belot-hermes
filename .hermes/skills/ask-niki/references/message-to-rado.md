# Niki's perspective — the original message for Rado

**Context:** This is the complete mentoring explanation drafted during the original Belot product-planning conversation, which Niki specifically asked to preserve as an \`ask-niki\` skill. It captures his documented approach; it is **not** a record of him personally delivering these exact words. This reference is intended for Hermes to teach the mindset with fidelity, not to claim live communication with Niki.

---

Rado, I want you to understand something important about how I approached this Belot project.

When I started, I had a simple idea: let's create our own Belot game.

I didn't know exactly how every part of the application should work. I didn't have a complete technical plan. And I didn't need one.

I started talking to AI, explaining my idea, and letting it help me turn that idea into something structured.

First, we identified what the game should do. Then we divided it into parts, discussed how people would use it, and made decisions about the most important features.

Eventually, I realized something: I was spending too much time making small decisions that AI could make for me.

So instead of telling AI what to do every five minutes, I started defining **how AI should think, make decisions, build things, and verify its own work**.

That's why we created the Constitution, the development processes, and the skills for Hermes.

Think about it like this:

**You're not learning how to program a game. You're learning how to manage an AI developer that programs the game for you.**

Imagine you have a developer working for you.

You tell him: "I don't like how the cards are displayed. I want it to be easier to see whose turn it is."

A good developer doesn't need you to explain React, CSS, databases or code. He understands what you're trying to achieve, comes up with a solution, builds it, tests it, and shows you the result.

That's exactly how I want you to work with Hermes.

You speak to him from Telegram. You describe your ideas, problems and frustrations. He helps you understand your options, makes sensible technical decisions and implements solutions.

Then you open the Demo version of the game on your phone and see whether it actually works.

If something is wrong, you explain what's wrong. He investigates and fixes it.

If you're happy with the result, you approve it for the Main version.

But there's something else.

I don't just want Hermes to build software for you. I want you to become better at thinking about products, technology, and AI as you use it.

Over time, you'll naturally learn what frontend, backend, databases, testing, AI agents and deployments are. Not because you're studying programming, but because you're building something real.

And remember: AI can make mistakes. That's why we created the processes for testing, verification and reviewing changes. We don't simply trust Hermes because he says something works. We check.

**Your main responsibility is to understand what you're trying to achieve and decide whether the result is good.**

Hermes takes responsibility for the engineering.

Start small. Explain what you want. Let Hermes guide you. Test what he builds. Learn something from the process. Then move on to the next improvement.

That's the whole idea.

And once you understand how to do this with Belot, you can use exactly the same approach to build almost any other software product.
